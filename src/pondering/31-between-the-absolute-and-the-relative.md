# Between the Absolute and the Relative

Recently, I have been thinking about the way human beings perceive reality.

Much of our perception seems to be relative. We understand one thing in relation to another: greater or smaller, higher or lower, better or worse, positive or negative. Even when we assign numbers to things, we understand magnitude and direction in relation to some reference point.

And this brings the often encountered apologetic question: **Can there be something relative without there first being something absolute, or at least some fixed reference by which the relative becomes meaningful?**

There is an interesting parallel in mathematics, science, and engineering.

Scientists often search for theories that can explain what they observe. Mathematicians look for underlying structures and relationships that remain true beyond a single example. There is a desire to discover something deeper than individual observations—a principle that can explain many different phenomena.

Engineers(yes, like me) encounter another side of this question: **precision**.

When an engineer measures or manufactures something, the physical object is almost never identical to its theoretical specification. There are tolerances, uncertainties, environmental conditions, and limitations in the measuring instruments themselves. We might say that a component should be exactly a certain length, but the object we actually produce can only approach that specification within some degree of precision.

Yet precision only makes sense because there is something we are trying to be precise **about**.

Measurement is meaningful because there is a reference. Error is meaningful because there is a target. A tolerance can be expressed because there is a specified value from which deviation can be measured. In a curious way, therefore, the recognition of imperfection already assumes some conception of what greater precision would mean.

This makes me think about our relationship with God.

Perhaps human knowledge is something like measurement. We observe, compare, reason, experience, and gradually refine our understanding. We can become more precise without ever claiming that our understanding is perfect. The limitation belongs to the observer, not necessarily to the reality being observed.

And perhaps this is also why the idea of infinity is so fascinating.

As human beings, we are finite. We cannot experience infinity directly in the ordinary sense, yet through mathematics we can reason about it. Through physics, we encounter realities whose scales stretch far beyond ordinary human experience. We can recognize that reality may be much greater than what our senses alone can contain.

Perhaps our knowledge of God is similar in one respect. We can genuinely know the existence of an infinite God, while there will always be more of Him for us to discover.

God is not simply a greater version of us. God is absolute. If He is infinitely good, infinitely powerful, and perfectly righteous, then His goodness cannot ultimately be measured against ours as though He were merely another point on the same scale. Rather, He is the One by whom goodness itself is known.

This leads me to an interesting paradox.

Our relationship with God can become **deeper and deeper**.

“Deeper” is a relative idea. It implies movement, growth, and degrees. I can know God more deeply today than I did yesterday, and perhaps more deeply tomorrow than I do today. Yet the God whom I am coming to know does not become more complete as my knowledge grows.

**God does not become clearer because He changes; He becomes clearer to me because I am being changed by Him.**

Perhaps this is somewhat like increasing the precision of a measurement. The reality itself does not need to move closer to my measurement; rather, my measurement becomes more faithful to the reality.

And there is another dimension to this: **time**.

As human beings, we live within time. We experience life moment by moment: yesterday, today, and tomorrow. Our understanding grows through this progression. We know more, love more deeply, become more mature, and learn to walk with God more closely.

In this sense, even our spiritual growth has a relative quality. We speak of becoming *closer*, going *deeper*, understanding *more clearly*, and knowing God *more and more*.

We are now creatures who experience becoming.

Yet God is not confined by time in the way we are. What we encounter progressively, He already holds in fullness.

Perhaps this is why our relationship with God can always deepen while we are here. It is not because God is gradually becoming more complete or revealing a better version of Himself. Rather, within the time we have been given, we are continually growing in our capacity to know Him, receive His love, recognize His goodness, and participate in His will.

This also helps me think about what it means to discern God's will in a particular moment.

God is absolute, yet I am finite. My understanding is limited. My perception can be distorted, and my own desires can interfere. Therefore, knowing God more deeply is not about making His truth relative to my circumstances. It is about becoming increasingly oriented toward Him so that, within the particular circumstances of my life, I can perceive and respond to His will more and more.

So there is something beautiful here:

**An imperfect being walking with the Perfect One.** <br>
**A finite being entering into relationship with the Infinite.**  <br>
**A temporal being learning to walk with the Eternal.**<br>

And perhaps this is part of the wonder of Jesus.

Through Christ's sacrifice, we are given the possibility of reconciliation and communion with God. Jesus becomes the bridge—not because the Absolute becomes merely relative, but because finite and broken human beings are invited into relationship with the One who is holy and perfect.

Through the Holy Spirit, we are not merely given abstract instructions about goodness. We are invited into partnership with God: to intentionally walk with Him, participate in His work, choose what is good, and experience His goodness in the particular circumstances of our lives.

In this sense, something extraordinary happens. We remain finite, yet we participate in something beyond ourselves. We remain imperfect, yet we walk with the Perfect One. We live within time, yet we are being drawn toward eternity.

And this gives eternity a beautiful meaning.

Eternity is not simply “more time.” It is the hope of entering fully into the presence of the One whom we have been learning to know throughout our temporal lives. What we now perceive partially, we will one day encounter in a way that is no longer constrained by the same limitations of our present existence.

So perhaps “more and more” belongs to our experience within time.

**God Himself is already fullness.**

This also makes me think about Eden.

Before humanity's fall, perhaps there was no need to understand goodness primarily through comparison with evil. Goodness was simply lived in communion with God. Humanity did not need brokenness in order to know wholeness; wholeness was simply the reality in which humanity lived with God.

After brokenness entered human experience, however, we became painfully aware of contrasts: good and evil, wholeness and brokenness, obedience and rebellion, life and death.

And perhaps the very fact that we can recognize something as broken points beyond the broken thing itself.

In engineering, to say that something deviates requires a reference from which it deviates. In the same way, to call something unjust seems to appeal to some conception of justice. To recognize brokenness suggests that we have some awareness of wholeness. To say that something *ought not to be this way* raises the deeper question:

**By what standard do we know how it ought to be?**

For me, that question ultimately points toward God—the absolute righteous One.

Perhaps this is one way to understand the tension between the relative and the absolute.

We live in a world of comparison, measurement, change, uncertainty, and time. Our perception is limited. Our knowledge develops. Our relationships deepen. We move from one moment to another, constantly experiencing “more” and “less,” “before” and “after,” “closer” and “farther.”

Yet within this relative existence, we are invited into relationship with the One who does not depend upon our measurements, comparisons, or changing perceptions in order to be who He is.

And Jesus is the hope within that tension.

Through Him, the finite is invited into communion with the Infinite, the imperfect with the Perfect, the temporal with the Eternal, and the broken with the One who makes things whole.

Perhaps, then, faith is not about reducing the Absolute into something relative enough for us to control or completely comprehend.

It is about allowing ourselves to be shaped by the One who is absolute.

And perhaps eternity is the fulfillment of the relative in the Absolute: the finite welcomed forever into the fullness of the presence of the Infinite.
