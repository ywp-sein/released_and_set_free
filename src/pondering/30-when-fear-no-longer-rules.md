# When Fear No Longer Rules

In recent months, I have begun to understand fear differently. Fear does not always appear as an obvious feeling of terror or anxiety. Sometimes it appears simply as a thought of uncertainty: *What if this does not work out? What if I cannot control what happens? What if I do not have enough?*

In this sense, fear can take many forms. It can be the fear of people and their opinions, the fear of an uncertain situation, or the fear of lacking something we believe we need. We may fear lacking material security, recognition from others, confidence in ourselves, or the resources necessary for what lies ahead. Beneath many of these fears seems to be the same question: *Will I have enough, and will I be okay if I cannot control the outcome?*

This has also made me reconsider frameworks such as Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. I do not believe Maslow’s hierarchy is a biblical framework, because it tends to understand human motivation through needs that must be progressively satisfied. Christianity begins somewhere different. If God is truly our Father, if He is good, if He knows what we need, if He provides for us, and if He remains sovereign even when circumstances appear uncertain, then lack does not have to govern our lives.

This does not mean that Christians have no real needs, nor does it mean that we will never experience fear. Rather, it means that our needs and fears do not have the final authority over us. The deeper question is whether we trust the goodness of God.

Perhaps, then, what we ultimately have to confront is not uncertainty itself, but our unbelief. Fear often exposes the places where I struggle to believe that God is good, that He is in control, or that He will provide what is truly necessary. When that trust is weakened, fear easily becomes convincing. I begin to interpret reality through what I lack rather than through who God is.

So what does it mean to be a fearless Christian?

I do not think it means becoming someone who never feels fear. There will be moments when fear arises, sometimes intensely. Fearlessness is not the denial of fear but a refusal to give fear our trust. We can acknowledge, *Yes, I am afraid. Yes, I do not know what will happen.* But acknowledging fear is different from obeying it. A fearful thought does not have to become the truth by which I live.

This is something I am learning: I can recognize fear without placing my faith in it.

Even when circumstances are overwhelming, and even when there seem to be many legitimate reasons to be afraid, the love of God can break fear’s authority over us. We may still feel uncertainty, but uncertainty does not have to destroy our peace.

Perhaps this is what a fearless Christian looks like: someone who does not necessarily know the outcome, yet can move forward in peace because they trust God. They do not need control before they obey. They do not even place ultimate confidence in their own ability to remain faithful; rather, they trust that God Himself will give them the grace and strength to obey Him.

There is something deeply counterintuitive about this. Human instinct tells us that peace should come after certainty: first know that everything will be all right, first secure the outcome, first eliminate the risk, and then be at peace.

But the Christian life seems to reverse that order.

We can have peace before we know the outcome.

And perhaps this is not a denial of what it means to be human, but a restoration of it. Perhaps our deepest human nature is not independence, self-sufficiency, or control, but dependence upon God. We were made to receive from Him: to depend upon His power when we are weak, His strength when ours fails, His love when we are afraid, and His goodness when we cannot see what lies ahead.

Fear says, *I need to know what will happen before I can have peace.*

Faith says, *I do not know what will happen, but I know the One in whom I trust.*

And as we learn to live from that trust, the goodness of God is no longer merely something we believe intellectually. It begins to shape the way we experience uncertainty itself. Fear may still knock at the door, but it no longer has to rule the heart. In its place, there can increasingly be peace, joy, and the freedom to obey God without first demanding control over the outcome.

P.S. There’s even that kind of overflowing joy that you don’t know how to contain. God is so fun and He is such a great Dad.
